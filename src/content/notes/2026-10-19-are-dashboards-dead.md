Some dashboards deserve to go the way of the dodo. The ones that show a small business owner something is wrong, then leave them to spend the afternoon figuring out what to do about it.

But dashboards as a whole? No. I want to keep the chart that helps me understand a problem. I want the software around it to take more responsibility for helping me deal with it.

## The chart is where the work starts

Imagine a small home-renovation company. This is a made-up example, but the work is ordinary: visit the house, prepare the quote, follow up.

The owner opens a dashboard and sees that fewer quotes are turning into jobs. That is useful to know. But now she has to open each quote, read the messages and ask around the team to understand why.

One customer is waiting for a revised price. Another already said no, but the record still says pending. A third asked the team to call after the weekend.

So she exports the quotes to a spreadsheet, adds notes beside each customer and works through the list. She sends the revised price and marks the declined quote as closed. By the end of the afternoon, the spreadsheet describes what happened.

But the quoting system does not. Next week, its dashboard sends her back to the same unresolved records. Her colleague opens one and calls a customer she has already dealt with.

She can update both places, or ask everyone to use her spreadsheet. Either way, keeping the software informed has become another job. The chart did its part. The product stopped short of helping her finish hers.

The dashboard found the question. The useful work happened when the team could answer it and do something about it.

## What should replace a dashboard?

For that owner, I would start with the quotes that need attention, with the reason each one is there and the messages behind it. Let her correct a bad suggestion without a fight. Once she sends the revised price, record it so nobody chases the same customer tomorrow.

Keep the chart nearby. She will still want to know whether the change is helping.

This is a direction I can see in existing analytics products, too. [Tableau Pulse delivers metric insights through email and Slack](https://help.tableau.com/current/online/en-gb/pulse_explore_metrics.htm), so people can notice a change without first remembering to open a dashboard. That is a useful step. It still leaves a product question: what can the person receiving that message actually do next?

Sending every wobble in a graph to someone’s phone would make their day worse. A small team needs to decide which changes deserve an interruption, who can respond, and which can wait for a weekly review.

## AI still needs to know what the numbers mean

Conversational analytics lets someone ask a question in ordinary language instead of finding the right filter. I want that option. I also want to see where the answer came from.

Ask whether sales are up. Do we mean accepted quotes, invoices sent, or money in the bank? Those answers can point in different directions. A confident sentence does not settle the definition.

[Microsoft’s guidance for preparing Power BI data for Copilot](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/power-bi/create-reports/copilot-prepare-data-ai-faq) covers selecting relevant data, defining business terms and configuring verified answers. The chat interface still needs people to establish what the data means.

That is why I’m wary of replacing a wall of charts with a blank chat box and calling the job finished. Now the owner has to know which question to ask, and enough about the business’s records to judge the answer. We may have made the screen tidier while asking more of her.

## Keep the view. Finish the job.

A dashboard earns its place when people need to compare trends, investigate something unexpected, or look at the same picture together. A list of overdue quotes cannot tell the whole story of a business. Neither can a chatbot’s reply.

But a person opening software to get through today’s work needs a way to finish it. Show them the relevant records. Help them make a decision. Remember what they did.

That is the standard I want to build toward with [tools for small teams](/building/). If the owner still has to copy everything into a spreadsheet before she can act, there is work left for us to do.

Pick one number your team checks every week. Follow what happens after someone reads it. Where do they go next? What do they have to piece together? Start improving the software there.
