I remember creating a financial dashboard for a plastic surgery clinic. It became a tool for the owners to share with their buddies.

Even when dashboards helped highlight problems, you still had to go and figure out how to solve them. Now it’s less about, “Here’s the problem.” It’s more about, “Do you understand what’s happening? Here are the ways to fix it.”

[Attio](https://attio.com/architecture) describes connecting context with action. [Sequoia](https://sequoiacap.com/article/services-the-new-software) argues for selling work rather than tools. But getting there takes more than generating another screen. With AI, you can generate pretty much any UI, any dashboard with almost no friction. That makes it easy to skip customer discovery: what does someone actually need to do?

<span id="what-id-change" aria-hidden="true"></span>

Take a monthly review of accounts receivable. I prefer a chart or a familiar screen for a routine like this. You need evidence. You need to quickly see if anything has changed, dive deeper into it, and decide how you should tweak your operating procedures. A conversation with your data could help you investigate. The chart still gives you a familiar place to start.

For me, you don’t just have a conversational interface. You’ll have different ways to do the work. Meeting people where they’re at means designing for where their organization is, and where they are when it comes to technology adoption.

It also means knowing where automation should stop. One of the biggest things I encourage is a personal follow-up after a person comes for treatment. Someone at the front desk, a care coordinator, should call them and ask how the treatment was. That relationship matters. I don’t want to hand it to an automated caller.

So before choosing what to automate, ask: where is your bottleneck, and where do you need to be involved? Sophistication is in the simplicity of getting from A to B.
